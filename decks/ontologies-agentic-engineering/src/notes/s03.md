Here is an industry case that made this concrete for me. On 16 October 2026 the Netflix Tech Blog published a post called Ontology-Driven Observability. It opens with a home screen regression. The home screen rendered about 1.5 seconds slower than before.

[next]

To investigate, the engineers had to work across five tools. They are Atlas, Radar, incident.io, PagerDuty and Spinnaker. Each one names the same service differently. I made up the service names on this slide to illustrate the problem. The post describes the pattern, and the names on screen are mine.

[next]

The investigation took about four hours. The post says an hour or two of that used to go into assembling context across those five systems plus Slack history. That time went into matching names, not into finding the cause. The people doing this were senior engineers who carry the mapping in their heads. They know that three different strings refer to one service, because they were around when each name was chosen.

[next]

An autonomous coding agent dropped into the same place has none of that history. It sees five names and has to decide whether they refer to one thing or five. It guesses. When it guesses wrong, it doesn't tell you, and the next step builds on the wrong answer.

[next]

Netflix's fix was to write the mapping down once. Every tool's name for the service now points at one typed entry in a shared catalog, so a person or an agent can ask the catalog instead of asking a colleague. The post says one query now answers a question that used to span three tools. That shared map helped the agent in this picture. It helps the people more, and that is next.
