The Netflix engineers were the ones paying for those five names, so stay with people for a minute. Working memory is small. Nelson Cowan's 2001 review puts it at about four chunks at a time. During that incident an engineer's four slots were full of names. Cognitive load theory, which John Sweller developed in the late 1980s, calls this extraneous load. It comes from how the information is presented and not from the problem. The actual problem, a slow home screen, is waiting for a free slot.

[next]

A shared model changes what counts as a chunk. Five names become one thing with one entry, and that takes one slot. Chunking is how experienced people handle more than beginners. Chase and Simon showed it with chess in 1973. Skilled players recalled real positions in much larger chunks than novices did, and most of that advantage went away when the pieces were placed at random. An ontology hands every engineer the experienced person's chunks on day one. The freed slots go to what changed, the cause and the fix.

[next]

The other benefit is that the chunks are the same in every head. Two engineers and an agent that all learned the system from one map can talk about it without translating. A new joiner reads the map instead of interviewing whoever has been here longest.

I want to be clear about the evidence. The memory research is old and solid. The claim that an ontology lowers the load on an engineering team is my argument from that research, not a measured result.

That shared map is an ontology. Before I unpack how ontologies work, let's take the first pushback every engineer raises: why build a map at all when the model could just read CLAUDE.md or search a vector store?
