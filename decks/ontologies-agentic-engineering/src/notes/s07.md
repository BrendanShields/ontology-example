Before I get to what an ontology can do, I want to separate that word from three others. Vendors blur them, and many arguments about ontologies come down to which of the four each person means. I'll go through them quickly so we all have the same picture.

The first picture is a taxonomy. A taxonomy is a hierarchy and nothing more. EnterpriseCustomer is a Customer. You can walk up and down the tree, and that is all it tells you.

[next]

The second is a semantic model. Data teams build these in Snowflake or Power BI. They standardize metrics, for example a NetRevenue SQL view. Everyone gets the same number for the same name. A semantic model says little about how things relate outside the metrics it defines.

[next]

The third is a knowledge graph. It holds connected instances, meaning specific things and the links between them. ADR_004 supersedes ADR_001 is a fact in a knowledge graph. It records what is true about two particular decision records.

[next]

The fourth is the ontology. It is the formal blueprint plus the rules. It says which types are allowed, which links between them are valid, and which logical constraints must hold. The knowledge graph holds the data, and the ontology defines what shape that data may take and what follows from it.

Tom Gruber defined it in 1993. He called an ontology "an explicit specification of a conceptualization". In plain words, you write down what kinds of things exist in your domain and how they are allowed to relate, in a form a machine can check. When I say ontology from here on, I mean the fourth picture, the links with their rules.

The rules are the part that pays for itself, so they come next.
