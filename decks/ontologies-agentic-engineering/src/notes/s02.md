I'll show three working examples you already use, and each one is about ordinary people.

Start with one person. This is Maya. On her own she is a profile, a row in a table.

[next]

This is Tom. Maya knows Tom. That one typed link between them is the whole idea of a social graph, and they both look pleased about it.

[next]

Now Priya turns up. Priya knows Tom. Nobody has told the app anything about Maya and Priya.

[next]

Watch the dot. It follows the first link from Maya to Tom, goes straight through Tom — to everyone's surprise — and arrives along the second link at Priya. That is what being a node in someone else's graph query feels like.

[next]

Two steps, so the app suggests Priya under people you may know. It didn't guess from names or photos. FOAF, which stands for friend of a friend, is the small vocabulary behind this. Because each node is a Person and each link is knows, a friend of a friend is an exact walk across the graph.

[next]

Second one: disambiguation. Tom says play Mercury.

[next]

There are at least three things called Mercury: a planet, a chemical element and a musician. A person works out which one from the word play. Software that only has the string cannot.

[next]

Wikidata gives each of them its own identifier and says what each one is. Q308 is a planet. Q925 is a chemical element. Q15869 is a human whose occupation is singer. The dashed part is the assistant's own guess — it takes play to mean it needs a musician. The typed facts leave one candidate standing. Schema.org does the same thing for web search, turning bare numbers on recipe and product pages into typed durations and ratings.

[next]

So Tom gets Freddie Mercury, and the assistant never looks up the weather on a planet.

[next]

Last one, and it is the serious one. Sam has had a heart attack.

[next]

His GP writes heart attack. The emergency department writes MI. The cardiologist writes myocardial infarction. A system that matches on text sees three different conditions for one man — the exact same naming problem Netflix had across five tools.

[next]

SNOMED CT lists all three wordings as synonyms of one concept, 22298006. Now Sam has one condition and he is counted once, in his own record and in every statistic built on it.

[next]

The concept also has parents. In SNOMED a myocardial infarction is a kind of ischemic heart disease, and a couple of steps further up that is a kind of heart disease. So when the heart clinic asks who has heart disease, Sam is on the list, even though nobody ever wrote those two words in his notes.

All three are working products. In each one a system made a guess about what a person meant, and an ontology gave it typed facts to check the guess against. Now let's separate the word ontology from three others it gets confused with.
